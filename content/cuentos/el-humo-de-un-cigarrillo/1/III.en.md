# III

Close to the sea, the diminutive Summer Palace stood out over the uneven terrain like a stone jewel set upon the crystalline aquamarine of the sea. Designed as a ducal residence barely two centuries ago, its decorative function glided smooth, continuous curved lines toward the flawless sky of the Night. Borrowing classical elements here and there, the slightly rebellious neo-romanticism that constituted it stayed up late into the night, gleaming like a diamond under the pearly light of the moon. The scent of salt crystallized around it, shaping its marine presence—weathered after years of sun and sea—and granting it that simplicity which is sufficient for Perfection. Inside, the salon was illuminated, emitting golden, temperate, and delicate radiations outward through the enormous and clear glasswork. They were like a mobile and living bonfire whose warmth—human warmth—radiated the essence of a joyful and friendly life to the outside world.

Lirio, invited to this dinner by the hosts more than a month ago, arrived dressed for a gala in an impeccable black suit—like obsidian—and a satin white shirt. The leather shoes reflected the undulating reverberations of the dim, autumnal orange of the lanterns. His tie was of dark silk. And as a guest of honor, he was enjoying a marvelous conversation, a fine glass of champagne, and the attractive attention of several of his interlocutors.

"Have you heard the latest statements from the Minister of the Interior?" asked Miss de Casas, guest of honor at the dinner and a great friend of luxury, with a certain tone of apathy.

"The same as always. As are those of the opposition. You know those politicians are the only ones who know everything without actually knowing anything at all. If only their voters had a little more discretion when boasting of their ignorance, but the truth is that, far from hiding it, they parade it like a medal or a privilege and shamelessly keep it in plain sight of all humanity. Apparently, the Enlightenment and Knowledge are of no use to exercise power; at least, that is what democracy consists of now. A curious way to confuse everything." It was Lirio who answered, exceedingly bored by conversing about something that had held no mystery for him for quite some time.

The ruby curtains surrounded the room, somehow making it cozy at their mercy, and the golden tone of the walls, reflecting on the pearly floor, colored it by extending brushstrokes of luxury embedded among the multitude and the smell of spices, wine, and music in the salon. An endless number of mysterious, restless, and uniformed beings slid their feet over the floor in a continuous dance that made them come and go ceaselessly into the kitchen, whose swinging doors turned and turned to the sound of jazz and laughter.

From the upper coffered ceiling, tiny iridescent droplets flowed, filtering through the diamond tears of the lamps and the reddish, electric fire of their candles. Nothing that was there was not beautiful. The soft burgundy of the wine successively turned into ruby, dark red, and bright blood as the light reflected inside the glass. The gentle undulations of the fluid exchanged luminous messages with the crimson surface of the lips that drank it.

On the central platters, the various foods stood out ready to be tasted; the central salad, full of the fruits of the saffron-colored earth and the clear, fresh, and unblemished green of the springs, bore sublime witness to the arrival of the diverse appetizers—desired, golden as the edge of the plates that served as their nuptial bed, and opening the delights of a heart endowed with that cautious and elaborate simplicity. There was nothing that did not flatter the taste. Nothing that did not dazzle. Nothing that could be wasted. Each glass of champagne gleamed frothier than the last, and each song sounded more deliberate, more alive, more real, as the alcohol diminished the sensation of having to arrive anywhere other than that bustling and exquisite salon. Inside it, there were no more places to go.

The cutlery was silver, the tablecloth pearl and silk, and the napkins gold and mother-of-pearl. The seats did not matter. The silk, the satin, and the personality of the guests made them disappear, relegating them to the status of mere utensils. All the glasses were of diamond and all the rims of emerald. In the Summer Palace, everything held the humor of joy and the murmur of salt.

"Tell me, Alina," Lirio began to address Miss de Casas with a certain air of offense, "do you think that one day you will fulfill your promise to attend a concert with me? Bear in mind that my time is limited, and that I die a little every minute I spend without your company."

"Oh! But Lirio, do you not remember that my only favorite concert pianist is you, and that is why I would only listen to you play the piano? Is it not true that you promised to offer me a private recital, under the auspices of the Night and beneath the umbrella of the Full Moon? Or is it that you only remember the promises made to you, and always forget the ones you must fulfill?"

"You know that when the sun sets, the wolves howl and emerge from their hiding places. Perhaps that is why you are so eloquent tonight. But I was thinking rather of a concert—for piano, to be more exact—although, honestly, I am dying to see Cleissy's new opera. Some say it is the first time in two hundred years that a masterpiece of entertainment has been written and produced."

"Oh! The truth is I don't expect much from it. Masterpieces are not what they used to be. For fifty years now, they have been nothing but pamphlets written in ten minutes. But you are in luck. I possess two marvelous invitations for its premiere in the city next month, and there is only one person with whom I would like to share them."

"I promise you, Alina, that I will kill all of humanity just to be the one who sits beside you that day."

"Come, come, Lirio. You know there is no need for you to go to such lengths. It is enough for you to get rid of Mr. Ramos. Not because he is my favorite, obviously, but because the pleasure that might cause me would be the best way to thank me for what I do for you."

At that moment, a curious-looking man, dressed in a tuxedo perfectly similar to everyone else's, seemed to be observing them. His thick eyebrows jutted from his face like two enormous firebrands, and his gaze, somewhat lost, could not seem anything but abject to Lirio once he had dug it into his face like two sharp claws. Perhaps the most striking thing about his appearance was that it did not attract the slightest bit of attention.

"Please, you know I will do whatever you ask, even if it means Mr. Ramos must suffer. And suffer greatly," commented Lirio, already nervous since the strange guest would not stop looking at him and whispering mendacious comments imperceptible to him from that spot.

"So be it, then. And perhaps in that way you will be more inclined to show me your own talents as an artist. You cannot fool me, I know you have them."

"Surely those who have sent you such flattery have been far too kind to me. Art is something too immense to fit inside me."

Midnight was approaching. The rhythm of the music did not wane, nor did that of the champagne. A black cat poked its enormous yellow eyes through one of the windows; patient, wise, and prudent, it perceived the warmth of the room without any hint of concern. A waiter approached Lirio with a sealed envelope on his tray. It was a note. He opened it:

"You are a dead man."

He looked around. He searched for the man who had been watching him all night. He was no longer there. Nor in the salon.
